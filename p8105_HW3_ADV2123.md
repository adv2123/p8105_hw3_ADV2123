The dataset is structurally tidy and has 2595176 rows and 7 columns,
with one row per station per day, from 747 NY weather stations between
1981-01-01 and 2010-12-31. Key variables are precipitation (`prcp`),
snowfall (`snow`), snow depth (`snwd`), and daily max/min temperature
(`tmax`, `tmin`). Missing data is a substantial issue, particularly for
temperature: `tmax` is missing in about 44% of rows, largely because
many stations do not record temperature, so temperature summaries
reflect only a subset of stations. This could result in average values
that are not representative of the true daily maximum temperature. The
same issue applied to `tmin`. The rest of the variables also have
missing values, although to lesser degree.

### 1.3 Two-panel plot: Mean tmax per year

``` r
mean_tmax_vs_year_plot =

  )
```

Average maximum temperatures are much higher in July (station means
mostly between 20 and 33 °C) than in January (mostly between -10 and 10
°C). January is also more variable, both across stations within a year
and from year to year. Within each month, stations tend to move
together, meaning that in a cold year, such as January 1994 or 2004,
nearly all stations are colder than usual, which suggests statewide
weather patterns rather than station-specific effects. There is no clear
long-term trend in either month over the 30 years.

There are several outliers. In January, individual stations were
noticeably colder than the rest of the state in 1982 (-13 °C), 1993, and
2005, while in 2004 a few stations were much warmer than the rest (7 to
10 °C when most were below 0 °C). In July, the outliers are mostly on
the cold side: one station averaged 14 °C in 1988, and another averaged
19 °C in 1984, when the other stations ranged up to about 29 °C.

The plot shows hexagons, each of which represents the number of
station-days with that combination of minimum and maximum temperature,
with lighter colors indicating more days. The plot shows a positive
association between the two, meaning that days with higher minimum
temperatures also tend to have higher maximum temperatures, forming an
ascending diagonal band. Observations are concentrated in two areas, one
around a minimum of 12–15 °C and a maximum of 24–27 °C (typical summer
days), and one around a minimum of -3 °C and a maximum of 3 °C (typical
winter days). There are a few suspicious values, likely resulting from
recording error. For example, some days have maximum and minimum
temperatures of 60 °C, and some have a minimum temperature higher than
the maximum.

Total daily activity generally declines with age in all three education
groups, particularly after about age 60. The decline is steepest among
participants with less than a high school education, falling from
roughly 17,000–18,000 MIMS in the 20s to about 9,000 MIMS at age 80. In
this group, men and women follow similar trends, with women slightly
more active at younger ages and men slightly more active between about
50 and 70.

In the “High school equivalent” group, activity rises to a peak around
age 40 before declining, and the peak is much more pronounced among
women (about 17,000 MIMS) than men (about 14,000 MIMS). In the “More
than high school” group, activity is fairly stable until about age 60
and then declines. In both of these groups, women are more active than
men at most ages.

It is important to note that each panel contains relatively few
participants, so few observations, specially outliers, can significantly
influence the direction of the curve. Also, one man in the “More than
high school” group has total activity near zero, which may reflect a
device that was not worn.

In sum, overall activity levels are broadly similar across the three
education groups, and the plot does not show a clear, consistent pattern
of higher or lower activity with increasing education. However, the
groups differ somewhat in how activity changes with age, and given the
small number of participants in each panel, this plot alone cannot
establish whether education is related to physical activity.

This plot shows mean activity over the 24-hour day for men and women
within each education level, with smooth trends added. All three panels
follow the same daily rhythm: activity declines after midnight to its
lowest point around 4 am, rises sharply from about 5–6 am, remains
elevated through the day, and declines steeply from around 8 pm. This is
consistent with typical sleep and waking hours.

The shape of the daytime period differs somewhat by education. Among
participants with less than a high school education, activity peaks in
late morning (around 11 am to noon) and then declines gradually through
the afternoon. In the other two groups, activity stays on a relatively
flat plateau from about 9 am until the evening.

Differences between men and women also vary by education level. In the
“High school equivalent” and “More than high school” groups, women are
more active than men during the day, and the gap is largest in the “More
than high school” group. Women in these groups also become active
slightly earlier in the morning. In the “Less than high school” group,
men and women have very similar daytime activity, while men are somewhat
more active overnight.
